Murabaha vs Conventional Loan: The True Cost in PKR

“Islamic financing is more expensive.”

I have heard this sentence in boardrooms, in bank branches and across my own desk for twenty-five years — from business owners who had just been quoted by both an Islamic and a conventional bank, and were holding two term sheets with two very different-looking rates on them.

So let me answer it the way a banker should: not with a slogan, but with arithmetic.

The honest answer is: it depends on what you are comparing — because the two products do not charge you in the same way. A conventional loan charges interest on the reducing balance: every month, interest is calculated only on what you still owe. A Murabaha charges profit on the original cost, fixed at the start, for the whole tenure. That single difference means two rates that look almost the same on paper can produce very different bills — and, just as often, a “cheaper-looking” Islamic quote can cost more than a “dearer-looking” conventional one. Sometimes the reverse is true. The rate alone tells you almost nothing. The total rupees tell you everything.

Let me show you.

How each structure actually works

The conventional loan

You borrow Rs 5,000,000. The bank charges interest — in Pakistan usually quoted as KIBOR plus a spread — on the balance outstanding. Your monthly instalment (EMI) is fixed, but its composition changes: early instalments are mostly interest, later ones mostly principal. As the balance falls, the interest inside each instalment falls with it. If you repay early, you stop paying interest from that day. If you pay late, additional interest — including, in many agreements, interest on the overdue interest — keeps the meter running.

The Murabaha

Murabaha is not a loan of money; it is a sale. The Islamic bank buys the asset or goods you need — machinery, inventory, a vehicle — and sells it to you at a disclosed price: cost plus an agreed profit. That selling price is fixed in the contract on day one and does not change, whatever happens to market rates afterwards. You pay it in instalments. Because the price is fixed, there is no “balance” on which further profit can accrue: the total you will ever pay is known, in full, before you sign.

Both structures end with you owning an asset and the bank earning a return. The mechanics — and the traps — are different.

The worked example: Rs 5,000,000 over 36 months

Take a typical SME requirement: Rs 5,000,000, repaid over 36 months.

  • Conventional quote: 20% per annum, interest on the reducing balance.
  • Islamic quote: Murabaha at 18% per annum — quoted flat, on the original cost.

Most owners would look at 18% versus 20% and conclude the Murabaha is cheaper. Watch what the arithmetic says. (These are example rates for illustration — your own quotes will differ, which is exactly why the method matters more than the numbers.)

Conventional loan at 20% (reducing balance)
The EMI formula gives a monthly instalment of Rs 185,818. Over 36 months you pay Rs 6,689,445 in total — of which Rs 1,689,445 is interest.

Murabaha at 18% (flat)
Profit = Rs 5,000,000 × 18% × 3 years = Rs 2,700,000. The selling price is therefore Rs 7,700,000, paid as 36 instalments of Rs 213,889.

Read that table again. The Murabaha carries the lower rate — and costs over ten lakh rupees more. The reason is no mystery and no trickery: 18% charged on the full Rs 5 million for all three years collects far more than 20% charged on a balance that shrinks every month. Run the equivalence and it is starker still: an 18% flat Murabaha costs about the same as a conventional loan at roughly 30.6% reducing. Conversely, for the Murabaha in this example to match the conventional loan’s total cost, its flat rate would need to be around 11.3%, not 18%.

Does this mean Islamic financing is always dearer? No — it means you cannot compare a flat rate with a reducing rate, ever. If the Islamic bank had quoted 11% flat against the conventional bank’s 20%, the Murabaha would have been the cheaper deal by a wide margin — and some days, in some banks, that is exactly the quote on the table. Profit rates move with each bank’s cost of funds and its appetite for your sector, just as spreads do. Compare totals, not rates.

What else changes besides the price

Ownership and the asset. In a Murabaha, the bank must actually own the asset before selling it to you — it carries ownership risk, however briefly, and the asset and its documentation sit at the centre of the transaction. In a conventional loan the bank lends cash against security and the asset is your affair. Practically, this is why Murabaha documentation asks more questions about the goods themselves.

Late payment. This is where the structures genuinely diverge. On a conventional loan, overdue amounts typically attract further interest — delay costs you money, paid to the bank. In Islamic banking, the bank cannot earn from your delay: late-payment amounts are handled through a charity mechanism under the bank’s Shariah governance and State Bank supervision — the customer undertakes to pay a specified amount to charity if he defaults, precisely so that late payment is discouraged without becoming income for the bank. Your credit record still suffers from default in both cases; what changes is who can profit from it.

No compounding in Murabaha. The Murabaha price is fixed at signing. It cannot grow because a payment was missed, and it cannot be re-priced if market rates rise. A conventional floating-rate loan can move against you mid-tenure in a rising-rate cycle — a risk business owners felt keenly in recent years — while a fixed Murabaha price will not.

Early settlement. Settle a conventional loan early and the interest simply stops. Settle a Murabaha early and you are asking the bank to give up part of a price you already agreed to — a rebate (ibra’). In practice, early-settlement rebates depend on the individual bank’s policy and applicable State Bank guidance, and they are not calculated the same way in every institution. Ask for the bank’s ibra’ practice in writing before you sign, not after you have sold the asset.

Before you sign: six questions to ask both banks

  1. “What is the total amount I will pay over the full tenure — in rupees?” Refuse to compare anything else until both banks have answered this.
  2. To the Islamic bank: “Is this profit rate flat, and on what amount is it calculated for the full tenure?” Then convert: total profit ÷ principal ÷ years = the true flat rate.
  3. “What happens, in rupees, if I pay one instalment thirty days late?” The answers will be structurally different — make sure you understand both.
  4. “If I settle in month 18, what exactly do I save?” Get the conventional bank’s prepayment terms and the Islamic bank’s ibra’ practice, in writing.
  5. “Is my rate fixed for the whole tenure, or can it move?” A floating conventional rate and a fixed Murabaha price are not the same risk.
  6. “What are the processing, documentation, insurance/takaful and valuation charges — all of them?” Fees routinely add more to the comparison than a quarter-point of rate.

The bottom line

“Islamic financing is more expensive” is sometimes true, sometimes false, and always the wrong question. The right question is: for this amount, this tenure and this asset, which signed contract takes fewer rupees out of my business — and which one’s risks can I live with? Answer that with totals, and you will never be misled by a headline rate again.
