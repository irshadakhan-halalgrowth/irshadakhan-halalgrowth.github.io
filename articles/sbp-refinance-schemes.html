SBP Refinance Schemes: The Complete Eligibility Guide for SME Exporters

He had qualified for the Export Finance Scheme for years, on his own export history and his own shipments. He had simply never heard of it.

A textile exporter came to see me a few years ago. Third generation in the business, looms running two shifts, orders from buyers he had served for a decade. He was paying close to fifteen per cent on his running finance, and he had come to ask me, politely, almost apologetically, whether there was any way to bring that cost down.

There was. He had qualified for the Export Finance Scheme for years. On his own export history, his own shipments, his own bank account. He had simply never heard of it. Nobody at his bank had ever mentioned it to him, because he had never asked, and the bank had never volunteered.

That conversation has stayed with me, because it is not rare. In my years on the credit side of the desk, I have watched good exporters pay commercial rates for years while a concessional scheme stood open to them, not because they were ineligible, but because nobody had ever sat them down and explained which scheme fits which business, what it actually costs, and what the bank will ask for before it says yes.

This is that explanation. No jargon where plain words will do, and every rate in it checked against the scheme as it stands in November 2026.

First, the honest context: the system changed

Before the schemes themselves, you need to understand one change, because it confuses almost everyone who asks me about this.

The State Bank used to refinance these schemes itself. It lent to the banks at a low rate, and the banks lent onward to exporters. Under the IMF's Extended Fund Facility, it was agreed that the State Bank's refinance schemes would be phased out over five years, from FY24 to FY28. So the plumbing changed: banks now fund these facilities from their own liquidity, and the Government of Pakistan pays the difference, the markup subsidy, through the Export-Import Bank of Pakistan, Pak EXIM.

The schemes did not disappear. The institution administering them changed, and the subsidy now flows through Pak EXIM instead of the State Bank. When you read "EXIM-administered" on a bank's page, that is what it means. Your application still goes to your bank. The bank still does the credit appraisal. The risk still sits with the bank.

And one number anchors everything in this article: the State Bank's policy rate is 12%, set by the Monetary Policy Committee on 26 October 2026 and effective from 27 October. Most of the scheme rates below are defined as a fixed distance from that rate, so when the policy rate moves, the scheme rates move with it. The next MPC meeting is scheduled for 14 December 2026. Treat every rate here as current as of November 2026, and confirm it with your bank on the day you apply.

Scheme 1: Export Finance Scheme, working capital for exporters

This is the workhorse. If you export anything, textiles, surgical goods, sports goods, rice, leather, food, and you need money to buy raw material, produce the order and ship it, this is the scheme that pays for that cycle.

What it covers: short-term working capital, pre-shipment and post-shipment, under Part I and Part II of the scheme. Tenor is up to 180 days, roughly one production-and-shipment cycle.

What it costs: the end-user markup is the policy rate minus three percentage points. At today's 12% policy rate, that is 9%. The bank's spread on top is capped: 1% for corporate exporters, 2% for SME exporters, so the 9% is a ceiling, not a starting point for negotiation. Compare that with a commercial running finance facility, priced off KIBOR plus a spread: six-month KIBOR was about 12.5% in mid-November 2026, so an SME paying KIBOR plus 2% is at roughly 14.5%.

How big is it: for the financial year 2026-27, the total Export Finance Scheme envelope is Rs 1,500 billion, up from Rs 1,000 billion. Inside that, Rs 300 billion is reserved for SME exporters and new exporters, announced on 8 November 2026. That reservation matters, because in the past the big exporters absorbed most of the limit before the small ones got to the table.

Who qualifies: exporters, manufacturers and service providers, with a genuine export history or genuine export orders. The scheme is governed by the State Bank's EFS guidebook and regulations, and your bank applies them. New exporters are explicitly included in the Rs 300 billion reservation, which is a real change from the days when you needed years of history to get in the door.

How to apply: through your bank, and since 1 November 2026, also through the State Bank's SME loan portal, SMEConnect, which took more than 10,000 applications in its first days. Apply through the portal or the bank; either way, the bank does the appraisal.

What the saving actually looks like

Take a typical case. An exporter draws Rs 50 million for 180 days to complete an order.

  • Under EFS at 9%: Rs 50,000,000 × 9% × 180/365 = about Rs 22 lakh.
  • On a commercial facility at 14.5%: the same 180 days cost about Rs 35.8 lakh.

The difference is about Rs 13.6 lakh on a single order cycle, money that stays in the business instead of going to the bank. Run three cycles a year and you are looking at roughly Rs 40 lakh. That is the difference between a scheme and a shrug.

Scheme 2: Long-Term Financing Facility, machinery for exporters

Working capital pays for the order. This one pays for the machine that makes the order possible.

What it covers: purchase of new plant and machinery, imported or locally manufactured, for export-oriented projects. New projects, and existing projects doing balancing, modernisation and replacement.

What it costs: the same formula as EFS, the policy rate minus three points, so 9% at today's policy rate.

How long: up to 10 years, including a grace period of up to 2 years. That grace period is the part exporters underestimate: two years before principal repayment starts, while the machine is installed, commissioned and earning.

How much: up to Rs 5 billion for a single project.

Who qualifies: export-oriented projects with export performance of USD 5 million or 50% of export sales. Financing is available for all sectors permitted under the Export Policy Order issued by the Ministry of Commerce. The scheme has run since 2007, and its Islamic version, the Islamic Long-Term Financing Facility, works on the same terms through Ijarah and Diminishing Musharakah.

On Rs 100 million of machinery, the annual markup at 9% is Rs 9 million. On a commercial term loan at roughly 14.5%, it is Rs 14.5 million. The saving is about Rs 5.5 million a year on the outstanding balance, every year of the tenor.

Scheme 3: Long-Term Export Growth Financing Facility, the new one

This is the scheme most exporters have not heard of yet, and it is the cheapest long-term money in the system. Pak EXIM announced it on 25 August 2026, and it is worth understanding properly, because its terms are strikingly better than the older facility, and its conditions are stricter.

What it costs: 2% for the first two years, then 5% for years 3 to 10. Read that again: two per cent. The government pays the bank the difference between the commercial rate (the policy rate plus 2%) and your end-user rate, as a subsidy, every quarter.

How much: up to Rs 10 billion for a single project, covering the C&F value of imported machinery or the ex-factory price of locally made machinery. Insurance, erection, commissioning and transport are not covered, only the machine itself.

How long: up to 10 years, with a grace period of up to 2 years on longer tenors (1 year for tenors up to 5 years).

Who qualifies, and here is the catch: two conditions, both of them mandatory. At least 80% of the project's sales must be exports, and the project must be a green project as defined under the Pakistan Green Taxonomy. Letters of credit established from 25 August 2026 onward are eligible. If the project does not yet meet the 80% test, financing can be given on projected exports, but the project must hit its export target within four years, and the fines for missing it are real: no penalty at 90% or more of the required exports, but paisa 28 per day per Rs 1,000 of outstanding finance if you achieve more than half the target, and paisa 37 per day per Rs 1,000 if you achieve less than half.

On Rs 100 million of machinery, LTEGFF costs Rs 2 million a year in the first two years, against Rs 9 million under LTFF. That is a saving of Rs 7 million a year while the machine is being paid for, and the reason the 80% and green conditions exist is precisely because the money is that cheap. It is not a facility for everyone. It is a facility for exporters who are genuinely export-led and genuinely green, and who can prove both.

The facility is available in both conventional and Islamic modes.

The Islamic versions: same economics, Shariah modes

For readers who finance Islamically, every scheme above has an Islamic twin, and the economics are the same:

  • Islamic Export Refinance Scheme (IERS): the Islamic version of EFS, offered in Part I and Part II through Murabaha, Musawamah and Istisna.
  • Islamic Long-Term Financing Facility (ILTFF): the Islamic version of LTFF, through Ijarah and Diminishing Musharakah, for the same plant and machinery, the same tenor of up to 10 years with up to 2 years' grace, and the same end-user rate, the policy rate minus three points.
  • LTEGFF is available in Islamic mode as well.

Ask your Islamic bank for the scheme by name. If the relationship manager does not know it, ask for the trade finance desk: the schemes sit there, not in the branch.

The change announced on 5 November 2026: read it carefully

On 5 November 2026, the Prime Minister directed that the requirement for SMEs to export at least 80% of their output to qualify for long-term financing should be abolished, and directed banks not to deviate from the concessional schemes. The same meeting noted Rs 300 billion under the Export Finance Scheme for SMEs and new exporters, and Rs 350 billion earmarked for long-term financing this year.

This is genuinely good news for SMEs that export part of their output, textile units, surgical goods, food processors, who were locked out by the 80% rule. But read it carefully: at the time of writing, the formal State Bank circular changing the rules had not yet been published. A Prime Minister's direction is not a circular, and a bank cannot sanction against a direction. Do not build your application on it until the revised instructions are issued. Check with your bank whether the circular has landed before you apply, and note that LTEGFF's published criteria still list the 80% test alongside the green test, so that facility may keep its own threshold even after the general rule changes.

Why scheme files still get declined

A scheme does not remove the bank's credit judgement. It changes the price of the money, not the quality of the file. The bank still does its own appraisal, and the risk still sits with the bank; Pak EXIM's own LTEGFF page says so plainly. So the same reasons I wrote about in my last article still apply here, with a few scheme-specific ones on top:

Export performance has to be proved, not claimed. Under LTFF, the USD 5 million or 50% test is measured on real shipments, real realisations, real documents. Under LTEGFF, the 80% test is measured on the project's own sales. If your export history lives partly outside the banking channel, it does not count.

The machinery has to be eligible. New plant and machinery only, for exportable goods. Second-hand machinery, machinery for the local market, and the incidental costs around the machine are outside the facility. Disbursement goes to the supplier, not to you.

The file still has to be one story. The financial statements, the tax return, the bank statement and the e-CIB report have to agree with each other. A scheme case with contradictions in it dies exactly like an ordinary case, sometimes faster, because scheme files attract more scrutiny, not less.

And the export target is a commitment, not an aspiration. Under LTEGFF, missing it triggers fines on a published scale and, on violation, recovery of the subsidy with a fine. Take the facility only for the exports you can genuinely deliver.

There is also a smaller, newer benefit worth knowing about: the Performance Based Rebate on Incremental Exports, effective 1 July 2026, pays a rebate of 1% on export growth up to 10% and 2% on growth above 10%, settled quarterly. It is not financing, but for an exporter growing year on year, it is real money back.

Before you apply: a short checklist

  1. Pick the right scheme first. Working capital for an order: EFS. A machine: LTFF. A machine for a green, export-led project: LTEGFF. Do not ask the bank for "a scheme" in general. Name the one that fits, and the conversation starts in a different place.
  2. Prove the exports. Gather the export realisation certificates, the shipping documents and the bank statements that show the proceeds coming in. If you are a new exporter, bring the orders, not the hopes.
  3. Check your e-CIB before the bank does. A scheme file with a surprise in the credit report dies like any other.
  4. Ask about the circular. If you are relying on the 5 November removal of the 80% condition, confirm the State Bank's revised instructions have actually been issued.
  5. Ask the Islamic question early. If you finance Islamically, ask for IERS or ILTFF by name, at the trade desk, not the branch counter.
  6. Apply through the right door. Your bank, or the State Bank's SMEConnect portal. Either way, the appraisal is the bank's, so walk in with the file already in order.
  7. Run the numbers yourself first. The bottom line

The cheapest money in Pakistan's banking system is not a secret. It is published, in scheme circulars and on banks' own pages, with rates, limits and eligibility written down in plain figures. What keeps exporters away from it is rarely ineligibility. It is that nobody explained it to them in time, or they asked for it with a file that could not carry it.

If you export, you are probably closer to one of these schemes than you think. Find out which one fits, get the file in order, and ask for it by name.
