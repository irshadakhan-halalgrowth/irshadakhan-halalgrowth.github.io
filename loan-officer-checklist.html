What Loan Officers Actually Look For in an SME Application

Most SME loan applications don't fail because the business is weak — they fail because the application doesn't let the loan officer see that the business is strong. After reviewing thousands of applications across a 34-branch SME portfolio, the pattern is consistent: the businesses that get approved aren't always the biggest or the most profitable, they're the ones whose paperwork makes the case for them clearly and completely.

The documents that actually matter

Beyond the standard application form, four things carry the most weight in an initial review: bank statements for the last 12 months (consistency matters more than peak balances), audited or self-prepared financial statements, proof of business ownership or registration, and a clear breakdown of what the financing will be used for. A loan officer forms a first impression from these four items before reading anything else — incomplete or inconsistent documents here slow everything down, regardless of how good the business actually is.

The ratios banks check

  • Debt-to-equity ratio. Shows how much of the business is funded by borrowing versus the owner's own capital — banks get cautious when debt significantly outweighs equity.
  • Current ratio (current assets ÷ current liabilities). A quick check on whether the business can cover its short-term obligations.
  • Debt service coverage ratio. Whether projected cash flow comfortably covers the proposed loan's installments — this is often the single number that decides loan size.
  • Account turnover and bounced-cheque history. Erratic banking activity or returned cheques raise more concern than a thin balance sheet.

Red flags that stall an application

A mismatch between declared business activity and actual account transactions is the fastest way to stall a file — if you say you run a textile trading business but your statements show construction payments, expect questions. Large, unexplained cash deposits just before applying are another common trigger, as is a business address that can't be verified. None of these automatically mean rejection, but each one adds a round of queries that slows the process down.

Where applicants get it wrong

The most common mistake I see is applying for financing before the business relationship with the bank has any track record — walking in cold with a large loan request rarely goes as smoothly as building a working account relationship first, even for a few months. A close second: owners understate their financing need to appear "conservative," then come back for a top-up within months, which actually weakens their credibility rather than helping it. Ask for what the business genuinely needs, back it with clean documentation, and be ready to explain every number in your own statements.
